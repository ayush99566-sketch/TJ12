God's Own Country

Rightfully called “God’s Own Country,” Kerala invites you to go with the flow. Drift through Alleppey’s serene backwaters, raft in wild Periyar, and wander Munnar’s emerald tea hills while savoring rich coastal flavors.

Ten days, nine nights, and every mile handled for you — from a private chauffeured SUV to handpicked stays and a night aboard your own houseboat. Be a guest in paradise with Tourrnival Jourrneys.

About This Trip

Delhi · Agra · Jaipur · Varanasi

The Golden Triangle is India's classic introduction — three cities, three empires, one unforgettable arc from Delhi's broad imperial avenues to Agra's marble monument to love and Jaipur's rose-pink palaces.

This eight-day route goes one step further, closing with two full days in Varanasi, where sunrise boat rides and the nightly Ganga Aarti carry the trip from history into something closer to devotion. A private AC SUV and driver handle every transfer along the way.

Day by Day

Your 8-Day Itinerary

A private driver meets you on arrival and transfers you straight to your hotel. In the afternoon, take in the ceremonial sweep of Rashtrapati Bhavan and India Gate before settling in for the night.

No meals included

The morning covers Humayun's Tomb — the garden-tomb that inspired the Taj Mahal a century later — and the soaring Jama Masjid. A rickshaw ride through the packed lanes of Chandni Chowk leads into the afternoon's stops at Qutub Minar and Gurudwara Bangla Sahib.

BreakfastLunch

Drive to Agra for a day built entirely around the Taj Mahal — the ivory-white marble mausoleum Emperor Shah Jahan raised for his wife Mumtaz Mahal, completed in 1653. See it in its best light, then spend the afternoon wandering the surrounding gardens and riverfront.

BreakfastLunch

A last morning in Agra takes in Agra Fort, the red-sandstone stronghold that housed the Mughal court until 1638, and its surrounding bazaars, before the road turns west toward Rajasthan and the Pink City.

BreakfastLunch

A full day through Rajasthan's capital: the pink lattice façade of Hawa Mahal, the stone astronomical instruments of Jantar Mantar, City Palace, the floating Jal Mahal, and the hilltop grandeur of Amber and Nahargarh Forts.

Breakfast

A short flight trades Rajasthan's forts for Varanasi's riverfront. Evening brings a walk along the ghats and a front-row seat for the Ganga Aarti, where priests perform synchronized rituals with towers of flame.

Breakfast

Before dawn, a boat ride drifts past the ghats as the city wakes. By late morning, Sarnath's Dhamekh Stupa and museum — home to the Lion Capital of Ashoka — trace Varanasi's Buddhist history, before an afternoon browsing the Banarasi silk market.

Breakfast

A final breakfast before your transfer to the airport closes out the trip.

About This Trip

Delhi to Varanasi, the Long Way

This is the long version of North India — thirteen days that trade a quick highlight reel for real depth, threading Delhi's imperial boulevards, Gwalior's hilltop fort, the UNESCO temple carvings of Khajuraho, and the moated palaces of Orchha before rejoining the familiar arc through Agra, Jaipur, and Varanasi.

A superfast train covers the Delhi–Gwalior leg, private sedan transfers handle the rest of the roads, and two short domestic flights close the gaps between Jaipur, Varanasi, and home — so thirteen days of ground actually feels like thirteen days of travel, not transit.

Day by Day

Your 13-Day Itinerary

Arrival before noon and transfer to the hotel. Late afternoon brings a drive past Rashtrapati Bhavan's tree-lined avenues and a stop at India Gate, the war memorial honoring soldiers of the First World War.

No meals included

Humayun's Tomb — a UNESCO World Heritage Site built in 1570 — opens the day, followed by Jama Masjid and a rickshaw ride through Chandni Chowk's bazaar. The afternoon covers Qutub Minar and Gurudwara Bangla Sahib, one of Delhi's most prominent Sikh houses of worship.

BreakfastDinner

A morning superfast train covers the run to Gwalior. Gwalior Fort — once called 'the pearl among fortresses of India' by Emperor Babur — anchors the afternoon, alongside Man Mandir Palace, Gujari Mahal, and the tomb of Tansen, the 16th-century classical musician.

Breakfast

The drive to Khajuraho sets up the next two days among temples built by the Chandela dynasty between the 9th and 11th centuries, renowned for their sculptural detail.

Breakfast

A full morning through the Khajuraho Group of Monuments, including the grand Kandariya Mahadeva Temple, is followed by an afternoon at Raneh Falls, where the Ken River has carved a dramatic red-granite canyon.

Breakfast

Orchha sits along the boulder-strewn banks of the Betwa River. The fort complex here — Jahangir Mahal, Raj Mahal, and Rai Parveen Mahal — is one of the best-preserved in the region.

BreakfastDinner

The road continues to Agra, the historic seat of the Mughal Empire. Agra Fort, a UNESCO World Heritage Site that housed the Mughal court until 1638, fills the afternoon, along with the city’s local markets.

BreakfastDinner

An early start catches the Taj Mahal at sunrise, its marble domes lit gold. En route to Jaipur, Chand Baori — a 9th-century stepwell that plunges 100 feet into the earth — breaks up the drive, before an evening walking tour through Jaipur's bazaars.

Breakfast

Hawa Mahal's five-storey lattice façade opens the day, followed by Jantar Mantar's UNESCO-listed stone astronomical instruments, the City Palace complex, and Galtaji Temple — better known as the Monkey Temple.

BreakfastDinner

Jal Mahal, the Water Palace floating on Man Sagar Lake, opens a day built around Amer Fort, Jaigarh Fort, and Nahargarh Fort perched on the edge of the Aravalli hills.

Breakfast

A short flight trades Rajasthan for the Ganges. Varanasi's markets and winding alleyways, along with its street food, fill the first afternoon.

Breakfast

A pre-dawn rowing boat ride along the Ganges leads into a morning at Sarnath, where the Buddha delivered his first sermon. The afternoon’s stop at Varanasi’s silk market gives way to the evening’s Ganga Aarti.

Breakfast

A final breakfast precedes the transfer to the airport for a domestic flight to Delhi, connecting onward.

About This Trip

Ghats, Temples & Old-City Lanes

Four days is enough time to see Varanasi properly — not as a day-trip stop, but as a city that rewards slowing down. This tour moves between the sacred and the everyday: dawn boat rides past eighty-four ghats, temple visits woven through the day, and an evening Ganga Aarti that never quite loses its power no matter how many times you've seen it.

A Sarnath excursion traces the city’s Buddhist history, while a heritage walk through Varanasi’s oldest lanes and a visit to Ramnagar Fort round out a trip built entirely around the Ganges and the life along its banks.

Day by Day

Your 4-Day Itinerary

A private transfer meets you at the airport or station and takes you to your hotel. In the evening, a private boat glides out for the Ganga Aarti at Dashashwamedh Ghat, where eleven priests perform synchronized rituals with towers of flame.

No meals included

A pre-dawn boat ride drifts past all eighty-four of Varanasi's historic ghats as the city wakes. The day continues on foot through Kashi Vishwanath, Annapurna, Kaal Bhairav, Sankat Mochan Hanuman, Durga, and Tulsi Manas temples.

BreakfastLunchDinner

A short drive to Sarnath covers the Dhamek Stupa, Chaukhandi Stupa, Mulagandha Kuti Vihar, the Ashoka Pillar, and the archaeological museum. Back in Varanasi, a guided heritage walk winds through the city’s oldest alleyways.

Sarnath Excursions

Ten kilometres from Varanasi, Sarnath is where the Buddha delivered his first sermon after attaining enlightenment. This three-hour excursion covers the deer park’s stupas, temples, and museum at an unhurried pace, with transport handled both ways.

Everything here is original to the site — a 1931 temple built by the Sri Lankan Maha Bodhi Society, a Bodhi tree grown from a cutting of the tree in Sri Lanka, and ruins that go back over two thousand years.

Ancient Stupas & Ruins

The Dhamekh Stupa rises 43.6 metres over monastery ruins that mark the Buddha’s first sermon.

The Ashoka Pillar Capital

Housed in the site museum — the same capital that became India’s national emblem.

Thai Buddha Vihara

An 80-foot standing Buddha statue, built over 14 years as an Indo-Thai cultural project.

About This Experience

Sailing & Camping on the Ganges

The Ganges has carried pilgrims, empires, and everyday river life for millennia, and this full-day sail covers one of its most scenic stretches — the run between Varanasi and the fortified hilltop town of Chunar. Aboard a traditional wooden country boat, with a boatman crew, a mattress-and-pillow sitting area under a canvas roof, and time to watch migratory birds and the occasional Gangetic dolphin surface alongside.

The day starts with a hotel freshen-up and breakfast before the drive out to Chunar, where a traditional garland and welcome drink mark the start of the sail. Lunch is served on board, evening tea comes with a stop on a river island, and the boat drifts back into Varanasi as the light fades — eight to ten unhurried hours on one of the river’s oldest-travelled stretches.

Migratory birds and, with some luck, a Gangetic dolphin surfacing alongside the boat are part of the sail’s everyday scenery.

Lunch, evening tea, and cookies are all served on board, cooked fresh in the boat's own onboard kitchen.

Garlands and a welcome drink greet every guest as they board at Chunar.

A sitar-and-tabla jugalbandi or a reading with a travelling astrologer can be arranged for the trip, on request.

No engine, no bench seats — just a wooden sailboat with a canvas roof, mattresses, and two boatmen at the oars.

Exploring the Soul of Varanasi: A Heritage Walk Through Time

A
ayushsingh99566 April 29, 2026 · 2 min read

It's not only a city, Varanasi. Along the banks of the holy Ganga, life and death dance together in this everlasting canvas that is a cosmos. This city is more than just a place to visit for an adventurer; it's a window into their soul.

Most visitors see Varanasi from a boat at sunrise and call it a day. We wanted something slower — a walk that lets the city's oldest lanes do the talking, led by guides who grew up hearing these stories rather than reciting them from a script. That's how our Heritage Walk took shape: part history lesson, part food crawl, part quiet observation of a city that has never stopped being itself.

Where Learning Meets Burning

Along the Ganges, Varanasi keeps every stage of life in plain view — naming ceremonies, weddings, and funeral rites all happen within a short walk of one another, often within sight of the river. Our guides don't rush past any of it. They pause at the ghats and temples along the route to explain what's actually happening and why it matters here more than almost anywhere else in the country, so what could look like spectacle to an outsider starts to read as everyday devotion.

A Walk Filled with Flavors and Stories

A walk through Varanasi is also a walk through its food stalls, and we treat that as part of the itinerary rather than a detour. Expect a stop for hot thandai, a paper plate of kachori at a stall that's probably been in the same spot for decades, and a paan folded to order by someone who's done it ten thousand times. Along the way you'll end up talking to the people who actually run this city day to day — shopkeepers, priests tending small shrines, silk weavers and artisans working in doorways — not just photographing them from across the lane.

Responsible, Real, and Rooted in Culture

We built this walk around small groups and local guides on purpose, so the money you spend stays close to the neighborhoods you're walking through and the people who make the experience worth having. The goal isn't a checklist of monuments — it's a real exchange between traveler and city, the kind that only happens when you slow down enough to actually talk to people instead of walking past them.

“Now we know why you said the soul of Varanasi lies in its lanes.”

Go Beyond the Usual Tourist Path

The standard Varanasi itinerary — a sunrise boat ride, a couple of temple stops, maybe a evening Ganga Aarti — is worth doing, but it only shows you the river-facing version of the city. The Heritage Walk turns inland, into the narrow galis where the everyday city actually lives: the workshops, the tea stalls, the centuries-old havelis that don't make it onto a postcard. It's the difference between visiting Varanasi and actually walking through it.
